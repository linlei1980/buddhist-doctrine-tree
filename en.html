Overview of the system

This page organises the foundational doctrines of Buddhism into twelve layers together with a main line that runs from the problem to the system. The main line has nine items in order: the Four Noble Truths, the three seals of the Dharma, dependent origination and karma, the Nikāyas and Abhidharma, Madhyamaka and Yogācāra, tathāgatagarbha, and doctrinal classification with the schools. Two further layers — Practice and History and Transmission — lie outside that line and cut across the whole: the first is doctrine carried into practice, the second is the historical formation of texts and schools.

The links of the main line take up one another's unfinished business. The Four Noble Truths establish what the problem is; the three seals supply the criterion by which a teaching is judged to be the Buddha's; dependent origination and karma unfold the principle; the Nikāyas and Abhidharma reorganise that principle into a systematic body of treatises; Mahāyāna opens out from there into the two great tracks of Madhyamaka and Yogācāra; tathāgatagarbha supplies the ground for why awakening is possible at all; and doctrinal classification and the schools arrange all of the above into the systems of the Chinese traditions.

Revision note
1. Time made into its own view.　In the first edition the dates sat scattered inside each node's “Dating · textual layers” table, so the line of development was hard to see. This edition adds a timeline arranged in nine periods, searchable and navigable, with 31 nodes marked as key.

2. Relation types normalised.　The first edition used 142 distinct labels across 223 relations, twelve of them simply the numerals 1–12 marking the links of dependent origination, so nothing could be searched by type. They are now reduced to ten: Part · Sequence · Cause · Identity · Assessment · Contrast · Practice · Layer · Basis · Note. The original wording is kept as small print on each relation row, and relations can be filtered by type.

3. Four structural supports added.
  • Layer 04, treatises — the councils, the rise of the eighteen schools, Abhidharma, the Sarvāstivāda, the Sautrāntika, the six causes and four conditions. The first edition cited the Kośa in 46 places while giving no home to the layer of the treatises at all; yet the scholastic debates are the precondition for understanding every school that follows.
  • Layer 05, Mahāyāna — Nāgārjuna, Madhyamaka, the Svātantrika/Prāsaṅgika distinction, Yogācāra, “consciousness exists, its object does not”, and Buddhist logic. In the first edition Madhyamaka appeared only inside a short lineage note on the Sanlun school, so one of the two great tracks of Indian Mahāyāna was missing.
  • Four contested questions — who receives the fruit of karma if there is no self; the atom and partless particles; whether the two truths are realities or teachings; whether tathāgatagarbha is definitive. The arguments that drove the theory forward went unrecorded.
  • Layer 12, history and transmission — Aśoka and the spread of the Dharma, the question of Mahāyāna origins, the four stages of Chinese translation, translation bureaus and matching meanings, the Tibetan translation project, the persecution of 845, the Southern transmission, and modern Buddhist studies. The first edition had schools but no history of transmission, and so could not answer questions such as why modern Chinese Buddhism is dominated by Chan and Pure Land.
4. Hierarchy aligned.　In the first edition the level marker of 57 nodes disagreed with their indentation in the outline (the eight consciousnesses and the transformation of consciousness into wisdom were listed as top-level concepts), and 5 nodes appeared twice. They are now placed where they belong, and the duplicated entries are marked “see also”.

5. Texts and dating.　Fifty-one text nodes have been added (one for each scripture, vinaya work, treatise or historical source cited by three or more nodes) together with a searchable canon index of 149 texts. A text node gives only its character, the scale of its citation here, and the nodes that cite it; it does not carry a date of its own — for a single scripture the origin of its teaching and the fixing of its text are often centuries apart, and that judgement belongs to the nodes that discuss the text (layer 04, layer 12).

6. Corrections.　The second item of the Southern “three characteristics” has been restored to “all formations are suffering” (the first edition mistakenly repeated “all dharmas are without self”, which left the four seals without the item on suffering); “nirvāṇa” is given consistently as “the peace of nirvāṇa”; the two titles of the Kośa are unified. Definitions themselves are left as they were: the depth of each is set by what the concept requires, not by a uniform length.
Reading paths and how to judge the material
Four ways to read
  • To build a framework — start from the main line on this page, then work through the outline from 01 to 12, reading each node's definition. In every node the first paragraph is the gist; exposition and disputed points follow.
  • To follow the development — switch to the Timeline and read from “c. 6th–5th century BCE” onwards, watching for the nodes marked as key.
  • To examine one concept — use the search box. It covers names, Indic terms, the body of the definitions and the titles of cited texts; enter Kośa or Madhyamakakārikā to list every relevant node and text node.
  • To compare school positions — filter by relation type. Selecting Contrast alone gives every disputed question; selecting Assessment gives each school's placement of a given concept.
What each node contains
  • Definition — organised as gist, exposition and disputed points.
  • Dating · textual layers — “Formation of the idea” is the earliest traceable form of the notion in the literature; “Fixation of the text” is when it was named and systematically arranged; “Chinese translation” is when a Chinese reader could actually encounter it. The three are often centuries apart, hence the separate columns.
  • Sources — cited as far as fascicle, chapter or sutta number, with the passage quoted; the tags distinguish scripture, vinaya, treatise, and historical source. A concept often appears first in a scripture and is fixed later by a treatise; conflating the two falsifies the dating.
  • Related nodes — with the type of relation and its specific wording; coloured terms inside a definition also link directly.
Three criteria for judging the dating
  • All dates here are approximate or given as a range. For the Buddha's dates there are three traditions — Southern (624–544 BCE), Chinese (565–486 BCE) and modern scholarship (c. 480–400 BCE) — and this page counts from “c. 5th century BCE” throughout, without adopting one.
  • Questions such as the councils and the origins of Mahāyāna are recorded only in later retrospective accounts, and those accounts do not agree with the chronology given by physical evidence (the Aśokan edicts, the Gandhāran manuscripts). Both sides are set out separately here.
  • The transmission history in layer 12 is a modern historical and philological reconstruction, different in kind from the doctrinal content of layers 01 to 11, and best read separately.
The network and the main line
  • The nine items of the main line mark the trunk only; they are not the only order in which the material can be understood. Of the 619 relations on this page about 49 per cent cross layers, and two relation types — Contested questions and Contrast — exist specifically to record doctrinal disagreement.

On the dating given here　All dates are approximate or given as a range, in three columns. Formation of the idea is the earliest traceable form of the notion in the literature; fixation of the text is when it was named, systematically arranged and given a settled formulation — the two are often centuries apart; Chinese translation is the point at which a Chinese reader could actually encounter it, frequently centuries after the Indian composition. For the Buddha's dates there are three traditions — Southern (624–544 BCE), Chinese (565–486 BCE) and modern scholarship (c. 480–400 BCE); this page counts from “c. 5th century BCE” throughout and does not adopt one.

On standpoint　This page passes no judgement on the doctrines; it records structure, definitions, sources and dating only. Where the schools differ over a concept (tathāgatagarbha, the two truths, unconditioned dharmas, the essence of the precepts), the divergence is marked at the node concerned and given its own “contested questions” nodes and Contrast relations, so that a reader can compare them directly.

On the textual layers　Alongside the early, developmental, folk and meta layers this page adds a modern layer — modern methods of study and modern forms of practice. Like the other four it concerns how the teaching has been arranged, studied and carried on, not the teaching itself. The translation history, Tibetan and Southern transmission, and scholarly discussion collected in layer 12 are material about the transmission and study of texts, and are not to be conflated with the doctrinal content of layers 01 to 11.
